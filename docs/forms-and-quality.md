# Forms, formulations, and product quality

## 1. The forms, ranked by evidence

| Form | Creatine fraction | Evidence for superiority | Cost per 5 g (example) | Verdict |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: | --- |
| **Creatine monohydrate** | 87.9% | Reference form; hundreds of trials | $0.15–$0.49 | **Choose this** |
| Micronised monohydrate | 87.9% | None for absorption; mixes better | $0.15–$0.34 | Fine; convenience only |
| Creapure monohydrate | 87.9% | None vs other pure monohydrate | $0.41 | Quality signal, not potency |
| Creatine HCl | 78.2% | None in controlled trials | $2.06–$3.12 | Avoid on value |
| Buffered (Kre-Alkalyn) | ~87.9% (pH-adjusted) | None; less muscle creatine at label dose | $1.39 | Avoid |
| Creatine ethyl ester | Esterified | Worse: converts to creatinine | — | Avoid |
| Creatine nitrate | Low creatine + nitrate | Nitrate may help endurance; creatine claim unproven | — | Not recommended |
| Citrate / malate / chelate | Lower | Insufficient comparative data | — | Not recommended |
| Gummies | 1–1.5 g per gummy | Convenience only; potency failures | $1.33–$2.22 | Occasional use at best |
| Blends | 5 g plus extras | No synergy; cost and complexity | $1.33–$1.67 | Buy monohydrate separately |

### Why monohydrate is the reference

Creatine monohydrate is the form used in the overwhelming majority of efficacy and
safety trials, including the long-duration studies underpinning the ISSN position
stand [R01]. It is inexpensive, stable as a dry powder, and delivers 87.9% creatine
by mass. Every alternative form is judged against it, and none has convincingly
beaten it.

### Creatine HCl

HCl is a simple salt with higher aqueous solubility. That is a real chemical
difference, but solubility is not the limiting step in muscle uptake. The critical
review of alternative forms concluded that claims of greater bioavailability,
greater muscle retention, or equal efficacy at lower doses are unsupported [R03].

Two recent trials agree:

- A 2025 placebo-controlled trial in elite handball and softball players found no
  significant difference between CrM and Cr-HCl on neuromuscular performance or
  body composition; only CrM improved the fat-free mass index [R07].
- A 2024 trial comparing Cr-HCl with two monohydrate dosing strategies found no
  advantage for HCl on strength, hypertrophy, or body composition [R08].

A 750 mg HCl capsule supplies about 587 mg creatine, so 5 g requires about 6.7
capsules. At $29.99 for 72 capsules, that is **$3.12 per 5 g** — roughly 21× the
cheapest monohydrate.

**Verdict:** not superior, much more expensive, and harder to dose.

### Buffered creatine (Kre-Alkalyn)

Buffered creatine is monohydrate adjusted to a high pH (marketed as pH 12). The
claim is greater stability and less "bloat".

The direct trial compared low-dose Kre-Alkalyn (1.5 g/day), high-dose Kre-Alkalyn
(20 g/day for 7 days then 5 g/day), and monohydrate (same loading protocol). Muscle
creatine increased in all groups over time, but the low-dose buffered group gained
least, and the pairwise comparison favoured monohydrate (p = 0.053). There was no
advantage in strength, body composition, anaerobic capacity, or side effects. The
authors concluded that buffered creatine is not more efficacious or safer [R04].

**Verdict:** the pH story does not translate into a benefit; cost per 5 g is high.

### Creatine ethyl ester (CEE)

CEE was designed to improve absorption by masking the carboxyl group. In practice,
it is unstable in the gut and converts to creatinine. In a direct trial, CEE
produced **lower** serum creatine than monohydrate despite being given at twice the
recommended dose, raised serum creatinine more, and did not improve body
composition or performance beyond training [R05; R06].

**Verdict:** avoid.

### Creatine nitrate

Creatine nitrate combines creatine with nitrate, which has independent evidence for
endurance and blood-flow effects. The products often contain relatively little
creatine, and there is no adequate evidence that the combination is superior to
monohydrate. Nitrate dosing and safety data come from a different literature [R03].

**Verdict:** not recommended; if you want nitrate, beetroot juice is a better-studied
source.

### Citrate, malate, and magnesium chelate

These forms are more soluble or claim better absorption. There is no body of
comparative trials showing superiority to monohydrate, and because the salt
fraction is lower, more grams are needed for the same creatine dose.

**Verdict:** insufficient evidence; not worth a premium.

### Gummies and liquids

Gummies solve adherence for people who dislike powder, but they have three
problems:

1. **Potency.** In a 2025 independent testing programme, 4 of 6 popular gummies
   contained little or no creatine; powders all passed [R46].
2. **Degradation.** Gummies that did contain creatine had elevated creatinine,
   indicating breakdown during manufacturing or storage.
3. **Cost and sugar.** Cost per 5 g is $1.33–$2.22, and a serving can carry 5 g of
   sugar.

Liquid creatine has a stability problem: creatine degrades to creatinine in
solution, especially with heat and acidity [R02; R53].

**Verdict:** a legitimate convenience trade-off only if you will not otherwise take
creatine; choose a certified product and accept the cost.

### Blends

Multi-ingredient products such as creatine + HMB, creatine + tart cherry, or
creatine + vitamin D are marketed as synergistic. The additional ingredients may
have their own evidence, but there is no consistent evidence that they enhance
creatine's effect, and the blend makes dose verification harder.

Examples in the database:

- Transparent Labs Creatine HMB: $1.67 per 5 g creatine.
- Legion Recharge: $1.33 per 5 g creatine.

Both cost 3–11× a plain monohydrate powder. If you want HMB or tart cherry, buy
them separately so you can evaluate each on its own evidence and dose.

## 2. Micronised creatine

Micronisation reduces particle size, which makes the powder mix more easily and
feel less gritty. It does **not** meaningfully change solubility in a way that
improves tissue uptake, and it does not increase muscle creatine retention [R02;
R03].

**Verdict:** a real convenience feature, not a potency feature. It is often free or
nearly free at the budget end, so there is no reason to avoid it — but no reason to
pay a premium for it.

## 3. Creapure

Creapure is a branded creatine monohydrate manufactured in Germany and specified at
very high purity. It is the ingredient used in much of the foundational research,
which makes it a reasonable quality signal [R02].

Two caveats:

- Creapure is an **ingredient** certification, not a finished-product certification.
  A product can use Creapure and still be manufactured or stored poorly.
- Other suppliers can also meet compendial purity standards. "Not Creapure" does not
  mean "impure".

**Verdict:** a positive signal, not a requirement.

## 4. Contamination and testing

### Heavy metals

A peer-reviewed survey of 33 creatine supplements found:

- creatinine was the major organic contaminant, exceeding 100 mg/kg in 44% of
  samples;
- about 15% had dihydro-1,3,5-triazine above the detection limit (maximum 8.0
  mg/kg), and some had dicyandiamide above 50 mg/kg;
- no thiourea was detected;
- among heavy metals, only mercury was detectable, below 1 mg/kg [R45].

That is reassuring on metals for that sample, but it is a 2011 Italian market
survey, not a guarantee for today's products.

More recent independent testing has found:

- **Powders perform well.** In SuppCo's 2025 programme, all tested powders met
  label claims and had low heavy metals [R46].
- **Gummies are the weak format.** Four of six failed identity or potency testing;
  one exceeded California's Prop 65 lead limit at 0.99 µg per serving [R46].
- **ConsumerLab's 2026 review** found one product exceeding its creatinine limit
  and concluded that cost did not predict quality; cost per 5 g ranged from $0.13
  to $1.85 [R48].

### What "contamination" means in practice

| Contaminant | Source | Risk | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Creatinine | Degradation of creatine | Indicates poor quality or storage; not acutely toxic at typical levels | Avoid products with high creatinine; buy fresh, store dry |
| Dicyandiamide / DHO | Manufacturing by-products | Regulated limits; long-term excess is undesirable | Choose GMP/certified products |
| Lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury | Raw materials, packaging | Chronic exposure risk; usually low in creatine | Choose tested products, especially for gummies |
| Microbes | Manufacturing | Spoilage or GI illness | Sealed, GMP product |

### Independent testing limitations

ConsumerLab is partly paywalled, SuppCo is a commercial testing programme, and
published market surveys are snapshots. No single source covers the whole market,
and a product that passed one lot may differ in the next. Certification programmes
reduce this uncertainty because they test ongoing production, not a single sample.

## 5. Certifications: what they actually verify

| Programme | What it tests | Frequency | What it does not tell you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | About 290 banned substances, label content, formulation review, facility inspection, annual audits | Ongoing, with lot testing | That the product is effective, or that it is the cheapest |
| **Informed Sport** | 285+ banned substances by ISO 17025 methods, every batch before release, facility quality review | Every batch | Efficacy, or that the ingredient is Creapure |
| **Informed Choice** | Retail monitoring and periodic testing for banned substances | Periodic retail sampling | Batch-by-batch release testing |
| **NSF Contents Certified** | Label content and contaminants (purity), not sport-banned substances | Periodic | Banned-substance screening for athletes |
| **USP Verified** | Label content, contaminants, manufacturing | Periodic | Sport-banned substances unless combined with another programme |

Sources: [R49; R51; R52].

### Who needs a sport certification?

- **Drug-tested athletes:** yes. Use NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport.
  Contamination with a banned substance is rare but catastrophic for a career.
- **Recreational lifters:** certification is a nice quality signal, not a
  requirement. A single-ingredient monohydrate from a reputable GMP manufacturer is
  usually sufficient.
- **Everyone:** certification does not change the molecule. A certified product and
  an uncertified but pure product should produce the same training effect.

## 6. Label accuracy and manufacturing

Dietary supplements in the US are not approved by the FDA before sale; the FDA
relies on post-market enforcement and GMP rules. That makes third-party testing and
reputation more important than label claims.

Practical checks:

- single ingredient, no proprietary blend;
- 5 g creatine per serving clearly stated;
- net weight consistent with servings × serving size;
- a lot number and expiry date;
- a certificate of analysis available on request;
- a certification listing that can be looked up by product name.

## 7. Storage and degradation

Dry creatine monohydrate is stable for years, even at elevated temperatures. In
solution, it converts to creatinine faster as temperature rises and pH falls:
about 4% at pH 5.5, 12% at pH 4.5, and 21% at pH 3.5 after 3 days at 25 °C [R02;
R53]. A 2025 study confirmed that temperature is the dominant factor in beverage
stability [R54].

Practical rules:

- keep the tub sealed and dry;
- do not store a mixed drink for days;
- if you mix with acidic juice, drink it promptly;
- discard powder that is clumped, discolored, or smells off.
