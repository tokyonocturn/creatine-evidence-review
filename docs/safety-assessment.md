# Safety assessment

## Bottom line

For healthy adults taking 3–5 g/day, creatine monohydrate has a safety profile
similar to placebo in controlled trials, including trials lasting up to five years
and a pooled dataset of 685 studies with 12,839 creatine users [R01; R35].

The most common real effects are mild gastrointestinal discomfort and a small
increase in body mass from intracellular water. The most common **false** concerns
are kidney damage, dehydration, cramps, and hair loss.

The people who genuinely need caution are those with pre-existing kidney disease,
those taking drugs that stress the kidneys, pregnant or breastfeeding women, people
with bipolar disorder, and minors — not because creatine is known to harm them, but
because the evidence in those groups is thin.

## 1. Adverse events in controlled trials

The largest safety synthesis pooled 685 creatine studies and 652 placebo studies:
12,839 creatine users and 13,452 placebo users, doses averaging 0.166 g/kg/day
(about 12.5 g/day), for a mean of 65 days and up to 14 years [R35].

| Outcome | Placebo | Creatine | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Studies reporting any side effect | 13.2% | 13.7% | No significant difference (p = 0.776) |
| Participants with GI issues | 4.05% | 5.51% | Not significant (p = 0.820) |
| Participants with cramping or muscle pain | 0.07% | 0.52% | Not significant (p = 0.085) |

The creatine group had a numerically higher rate of GI complaints and cramping, but
the differences were not statistically robust when participant-level data were
pooled. In practical terms: some people get loose stools or bloating, especially
during loading, and splitting the dose or taking it with food solves most cases.

## 2. Kidney function

### What actually happens

Creatine is spontaneously and irreversibly converted to creatinine at roughly 1–2%
of the body pool per day [R53]. Supplementing creatine increases the amount of
creatinine produced, so **serum creatinine rises** even when the kidneys are
working perfectly.

Two 2026 meta-analyses quantify this:

| Marker | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Serum creatinine | +0.14 mg/dL (95% CI 0.05–0.22) | [R38] |
| eGFR estimated from creatinine | -10.75 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI -17.48 to -4.02) | [R38] |
| eGFR by chromium-51 EDTA (direct tracer) | +5.89 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI -0.30 to 12.08, not significant) | [R38] |
| Serum urea | No significant change | [R37; R38] |
| Albuminuria / proteinuria | No significant change | [R38] |
| eGFR (creatinine-based) in a separate meta-analysis | -5.20 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI -15.00 to 4.60, not significant) | [R37] |

The apparent contradiction between the two eGFR estimates is informative. The
creatinine-based estimate falls because creatinine rises; the direct tracer
measurement does not. A 12-week trial in resistance-trained men eating a high-protein
diet found no change in chromium-51 EDTA clearance, creatinine clearance, urea,
electrolytes, proteinuria, or albuminuria [R39].

### The clinical trap

A person taking creatine can be misclassified as having chronic kidney disease
because automated eGFR equations assume creatinine comes only from muscle. This is
a **diagnostic artefact**, not kidney injury.

If you take creatine and need kidney function assessed:

- Tell the clinician and lab before the test.
- Ask whether cystatin C or a direct tracer measurement is more appropriate.
- Do not stop prescribed monitoring or medication on your own.
- A rise in creatinine without other signs of kidney injury should not be treated
  as kidney disease until confirmed by a non-creatinine method.

### Pre-existing kidney disease

Trials largely excluded people with kidney disease. Case reports of renal
deterioration exist but are confounded by pre-existing disease, dehydration,
nephrotoxic drugs, or extreme doses. The American Kidney Fund and several reviews
recommend caution and clinician involvement rather than routine use [R36; R40].

**Verdict:** safe in healthy kidneys; avoid or supervise in chronic kidney disease.

## 3. Liver function

No controlled trial has shown a clinically meaningful adverse effect on liver
enzymes. A large US population analysis found no association between dietary
creatine intake and liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, or hepatic steatosis, and some
favorable associations with individual liver tests [R63]. Safety reviews reach the
same conclusion [R40].

Caveat: people with liver disease were largely excluded from trials, so the
evidence applies to healthy livers.

**Verdict:** no signal of harm; insufficient evidence in established liver disease.

## 4. Hydration, thermoregulation, and cramps

Creatine increases **intracellular** water, not extracellular fluid. Total body
water may rise by about 0.8–1.5 L during loading, but plasma volume, sweat rate,
electrolytes, and core temperature do not worsen [R40; R41].

- A meta-analysis of 10 studies found no evidence that creatine impairs heat
  dissipation or body-fluid balance [R41].
- In dehydrated men exercising in the heat, creatine did not increase symptoms or
  compromise thermoregulation [R41].
- Some data suggest creatine may *help* maintain hydration and reduce cramping,
  though this is less certain [R40].

**Verdict:** the dehydration and cramping claims are not supported. Drink to thirst
as usual; creatine does not require extra fluid beyond normal needs.

## 5. Body weight and body composition

Loading typically adds **0.8–1.5 kg** within a week, and long-term use is
associated with a 1–2 kg increase [R34; R40]. This is mostly water held inside
muscle cells, plus lean tissue over time. Fat mass does not increase; some trials
show a small decrease [R12].

This matters for athletes in weight-class sports and for anyone who monitors scale
weight closely. The gain is not "water bloat" in the subcutaneous sense and usually
settles.

## 6. Gastrointestinal effects

GI symptoms are dose-dependent and more common during loading. Strategies:

- split 20 g into 4–5 doses of 5 g;
- take with food or a carbohydrate-containing drink;
- use cold water and drink promptly;
- reduce to 3–5 g/day if symptoms persist.

There is no consistent evidence that HCl or buffered creatine causes less GI upset
than monohydrate; the 2012 buffered trial explicitly found no reduction in side
effects [R04].

## 7. Hair loss and DHT

This is the most persistent modern myth.

**The origin:** a 2009 crossover trial in 20 college rugby players found that DHT
rose 56% after 7 days of loading (25 g/day) and remained 40% above baseline after
21 days, with a 36% rise in the DHT:testosterone ratio [R44]. The study did **not**
measure hair loss, hair density, or follicle health. Baseline DHT was also 23% lower
in the creatine group, which inflates the apparent change [R02].

**The direct test:** a 2025 randomised controlled trial gave 38 men 5 g/day for 12
weeks and measured DHT, testosterone, the DHT:T ratio, hair density, follicular
unit count, and cumulative hair thickness. There were no significant differences
between creatine and placebo for any hormone or hair outcome [R43].

**Verdict:** the claim that creatine causes hair loss is unsupported. A transient
DHT change at loading doses is possible, but it has not been shown to affect hair,
and the only trial designed to measure hair found no effect.

## 8. Cancer

No human evidence shows that creatine increases cancer risk [R40]. Animal and
in vitro studies have produced mixed mechanistic signals, including some
anticancer effects, but these do not establish human risk. The theoretical concern
about carcinogenic degradation products has not been borne out at typical intakes.

## 9. Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Human safety data are insufficient. Creatine is actively studied as a potential
intervention in pregnancy, but that is experimental research, not a safety
endorsement [R21]. The safest position is: do not self-supplement during pregnancy
or breastfeeding without clinician guidance.

## 10. Children and adolescents

Short-term creatine use in supervised adolescent athletes has not raised safety
signals, and the ISSN position stand does not identify a specific hazard [R01; R02].
However, long-term data are lacking, growth and maturation are special
considerations, and the ethical bar for supplementing minors is higher.

**Verdict:** not a default; use only with guardian and clinician involvement, and
prefer food and training first.

## 11. Bipolar disorder and mental health

Case reports and clinical guidance suggest caution in bipolar disorder because
creatine's effects on brain energy metabolism could, in theory, destabilise mood
[R40]. The evidence is case-level, not trial-level, but the potential downside
warrants clinician involvement.

## 12. Medications and drug interactions

A 2026 evidence-mapping review searched interaction databases and the published
literature and found **no controlled human studies** directly evaluating
creatine–drug interactions. No moderate- or high-severity interaction is
established; reported signals are minor and theoretical, mostly related to renal
excretion [R42].

| Drug class | Concern | Practical advice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) | Additive kidney stress, especially with dehydration | Occasional use is fine; chronic use plus creatine warrants clinician review |
| Diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide, furosemide) | Fluid and electrolyte shifts; kidney load | Discuss with prescriber; monitor hydration |
| ACE inhibitors / ARBs | Renoprotective but affect renal haemodynamics | Generally compatible in healthy people; monitor if kidney disease |
| Nephrotoxic drugs (cyclosporine, aminoglycosides, lithium) | Theoretical additive risk | Avoid unsupervised creatine |
| Caffeine / stimulants | Possible attenuation at high doses (≥5 mg/kg); no consistent harm | Normal coffee intake is fine; avoid stacking high-dose stimulants |
| Parkinson's medication | In one large trial, high caffeine intake was associated with faster progression; secondary and hypothesis-generating | Do not change treatment; discuss with neurologist |

The Parkinson's caffeine observation is from a secondary analysis, not a
randomised comparison, and should not be read as proof that caffeine or creatine
harms Parkinson's patients [R28].

## 13. Kidney stones

There is no consistent evidence that creatine increases kidney stone risk in
healthy people at recommended doses [R36; R40]. People with a history of stones
should still maintain good hydration and discuss supplementation with a clinician.

## 14. Contamination as a safety issue

The largest safety risk from creatine is not the molecule; it is a poorly made
product. Heavy-metal contamination, underdosing, and degradation to creatinine
have all been documented, especially in gummies (see
[product quality](forms-and-quality.md#contamination-and-testing)).

**Mitigation:** choose a single-ingredient monohydrate powder with a documented
certification or a published certificate of analysis. Certification is not a
guarantee of efficacy, but it reduces contamination and label risk.

| Concern | Evidence | Magnitude | Bottom line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GI upset | Consistent, dose-dependent | Mild, 5–6% | Real but manageable |
| Weight gain | Consistent | 0.8–2 kg | Water and lean mass, not fat |
| Serum creatinine rise | Consistent | +0.13–0.14 mg/dL | Expected, not injury |
| Measured GFR decline | Not present with tracer | None | Lab artefact with creatinine |

## 16. Who should not self-supplement

- Chronic kidney disease, dialysis, or a kidney transplant.
- A single kidney or known renal structural disease, unless a nephrologist agrees.
- Taking cyclosporine, lithium, aminoglycosides, or high-dose NSAIDs regularly.
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Bipolar disorder or a history of mania, without psychiatric guidance.
- Under 18, without guardian and clinician involvement.
- Known creatine transporter deficiency or a metabolic disorder affecting creatine
  metabolism.
- Any unexplained rise in creatinine or liver enzymes — investigate first.

## 17. If you take creatine and need blood work

1. Tell the clinician and the lab.
2. Ask for cystatin C or a non-creatinine GFR estimate if kidney function is the
   question.
3. Do not interpret an isolated creatinine rise as kidney disease.
4. Do not stop prescribed medicines or monitoring without advice.
5. Bring the product label so the dose and form are known.
