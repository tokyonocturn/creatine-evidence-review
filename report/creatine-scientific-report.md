# Creatine Supplementation: An Independent Scientific Review of Efficacy, Safety, Formulations, and Product Value

**Version 1.0 · 18 November 2026**

Kevin Edwards · Independent evidence review · No industry funding or affiliate relationships

---

## Abstract

**Background.** Creatine is the most studied legal ergogenic supplement, but its
public reputation is shaped by two opposite distortions: claims that it is a
near-miraculous compound for muscle, brain, and longevity, and fears that it
damages kidneys, causes hair loss, or is a steroid. Both are wrong in different
ways. This review separates the established physiology from the marketing.

**Methods.** Structured review of randomised controlled trials, systematic reviews,
meta-analyses, position stands, government evidence reviews, independent laboratory
testing, and certifier documentation, prioritising 2015–2026 syntheses. Fifty-nine
evidence rows were graded for certainty and status; thirty common claims were
adjudicated; twenty products were priced and normalised to cost per 5 g of creatine.
Datasets and generation code are published with this report.

**Results.** Creatine monohydrate reliably improves strength and power: pooled
effects include about +4.4 kg upper-body one-repetition maximum (1RM), +11.4 kg leg
press 1RM, and +48 W Wingate peak power versus placebo plus training, with smaller
estimates in broader syntheses (+1.4 kg bench, +5.6 kg squat). With resistance
training it adds about 1.1 kg lean mass and reduces fat mass by about 0.7 kg over a
training block. Without training, the lean-mass effect is approximately zero.
Cognitive effects are small and most consistent for memory in older adults; the
evidence in healthy young adults is weak. Alternative forms — HCl, buffered,
ethyl ester, nitrate — are not superior to monohydrate, and several are materially
worse or far more expensive. Dosing is 3–5 g/day (about 0.03 g/kg); loading at
20 g/day for 5–7 days is optional. In healthy adults, safety data up to five years
and a pooled dataset of 685 studies show an adverse-event profile similar to
placebo. Serum creatinine rises and creatinine-based eGFR falls, but direct tracer
GFR does not change: the kidney signal is largely a measurement artefact.
Product quality varies: powders generally pass independent testing, while several
creatine gummies have contained little or no creatine. Cost per 5 g ranges from
$0.15 to $3.12 — a roughly 21-fold spread for the same molecule.

**Conclusions.** Creatine monohydrate is genuinely beneficial for strength, power,
and training-induced lean mass, at a low cost and with a favorable safety profile
in healthy people. It is not a fat burner, a replacement for training, or a proven
cognitive enhancer for the general population. Most consumers should buy a
single-ingredient monohydrate powder, ideally with a sport certification if they
are drug-tested, and take 3–5 g daily. The premium for HCl, buffered creatine,
blends, and gummies buys convenience or marketing, not efficacy.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Why creatine, and why another review

Creatine has been studied for more than a century and used as an ergogenic aid
since the early 1990s. The volume of evidence is extraordinary: hundreds of
randomised trials, dozens of meta-analyses, position stands from the International
Society of Sports Nutrition, and clinical research spanning neuromuscular disease,
depression, traumatic brain injury, and healthy ageing.

That volume creates its own problem. A reader can find a study to support almost
any claim, and the marketing layer — HCl, buffered, "superior absorption",
"cognitive creatine", "creatine gummies" — has grown faster than the evidence. The
2026 umbrella review of creatine syntheses found favourable effects on selected
strength, power, lean-mass, and physical-function outcomes, but also that **no
review achieved high AMSTAR-2 confidence and nearly half were rated critically
low** [R16]. The primary studies are heavily reused across reviews: 395 unique
studies accounted for 1,001 study–review inclusions. Concordance across reviews is
therefore not independent replication.

This review takes a different approach from a narrative supplement article. It
grades each claim, publishes the underlying rows, normalises product costs to the
dose that matters, and explicitly separates what is established from what is
emerging, mixed, or unsupported.

### 1.2 What this review covers

- creatine monohydrate versus HCl, buffered creatine, ethyl ester, nitrate,
  citrate/malate, chelate, gummies, and blends;
- strength, power, sprint, endurance, recovery, and muscle growth;
- cognitive, neurological, and other health outcomes;
- dose, loading, timing, and consistency;
- long-term safety, kidney and liver markers, water retention, hair loss, and
  other commonly discussed risks;
- hydration, digestion, and body weight;
- sex and age differences;
- product purity, contamination, manufacturing, and certification;
- brand pricing and cost per effective daily dose;
- medication interactions and medical precautions.

### 1.3 How to read the evidence grades

Each claim in the [evidence tables](../docs/generated/evidence-tables.md) carries a
**certainty** grade (High, Moderate, Low, Very low) and a **status** (Established,
Emerging, Mixed, Unsupported). Certainty describes how much confidence to place in
the estimate. Status describes whether the claim is settled enough to act on.

A useful discipline: ask three questions of any creatine claim.

1. **What is the comparison?** Creatine versus placebo, or creatine plus training
   versus training alone? The second is the relevant question for hypertrophy.
2. **What is the outcome?** A 1RM strength test, a muscle biopsy, a cognitive task,
   or a self-reported feeling are not interchangeable.
3. **Who was studied?** Healthy young men, older women, vegetarians, trained
   athletes, and clinical populations respond differently.

## 2. What creatine is and does

### 2.1 Chemistry and physiology

Creatine (methylguanidino acetic acid) is a naturally occurring nitrogenous
compound synthesised endogenously from arginine, glycine, and methionine, and
obtained from meat and fish. A typical omnivorous diet supplies about 1–2 g/day;
the body synthesises roughly 1 g/day. The total body pool is about 120–140 g in a
70 kg adult, and about 95% is stored in skeletal muscle, with the remainder in
brain, heart, and other tissues [R01; R53].

Inside muscle, creatine is phosphorylated to **phosphocreatine (PCr)** by creatine
kinase. The PCr/creatine kinase system buffers ATP during short, intense efforts:
PCr donates a phosphate to ADP to regenerate ATP faster than glycolysis or
oxidative phosphorylation can. This is why creatine matters most for efforts lasting
seconds to a few minutes — heavy sets, sprints, jumps, repeated high-intensity
efforts — and much less for steady-state endurance.

Creatine also has non-energetic roles that plausibly contribute to hypertrophy:
it increases intracellular water (a cellular hydration signal), may influence
protein synthesis signalling, satellite cell activity, glycogen storage, calcium
handling, and inflammation [R01; R12]. These mechanisms are real but should not be
over-sold; the hypertrophy effect is modest and depends on training.

### 2.2 Saturation

On a habitual diet, muscle creatine stores are about 60–80% saturated. Supplementation
raises muscle creatine and PCr by roughly 20–40%, with an upper storage limit near
160 mmol/kg dry muscle [R01].

Two protocols reach that plateau:

- **Loading:** 20–25 g/day (about 0.3 g/kg) in divided doses for 5–7 days.
- **Low-dose:** 3 g/day for about 28 days.

The classic comparison found similar muscle creatine accumulation after 3 g/day for
28 days and 20 g/day for 6 days [R01; R02]. Loading is faster, not more effective in
the long run.

### 2.3 Responders and non-responders

Response varies. In a small loading study, 3 of 11 men were classified as
responders (+29.5 mmol/kg dry muscle), 5 as quasi-responders (+14.9), and 3 as
non-responders (+5.1) [R32]. Responders tended to have lower baseline muscle
creatine, a higher proportion of type II fibres, and more fat-free mass.

The frequently repeated claim that "30% of people are non-responders" is not a
population estimate. It comes from very small samples with arbitrary cut-offs.
Some people gain less; almost no one gains nothing in performance terms if they
train and take an adequate dose. Vegetarians and vegans start lower and therefore
have more room to gain [R31].

## 3. Formulations: monohydrate and its competitors

### 3.1 The short version

**Creatine monohydrate is the reference form.** It is 87.9% creatine by mass, cheap,
stable as a dry powder, and used in the overwhelming majority of efficacy and safety
trials. No alternative has convincingly beaten it.

| Form | Creatine by mass | Claimed advantage | What the evidence shows | Cost per 5 g |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
| Monohydrate | 87.9% | Reference | Hundreds of trials; effective and safe | $0.15–$0.49 |
| Micronised monohydrate | 87.9% | Mixes better | Mixes better; no absorption advantage | $0.15–$0.34 |
| Creapure monohydrate | 87.9% | Higher purity | Quality ingredient; no potency advantage | $0.41 |
| Creatine HCl | 78.2% | More soluble, lower dose | No advantage in controlled trials | $2.06–$3.12 |
| Buffered (Kre-Alkalyn) | ~87.9% | Less bloat, more stable | No advantage; lower muscle creatine at label dose | $1.39 |
| Creatine ethyl ester | Esterified | Better absorption | Converts to creatinine; worse than monohydrate | — |
| Creatine nitrate | Low | Nitrate + creatine synergy | Nitrate has separate evidence; combination unproven | — |
| Citrate / malate / chelate | Lower | Solubility, absorption | Insufficient comparative evidence | — |
| Gummies | 1–1.5 g/unit | Convenience | Potency failures; expensive | $1.33–$2.22 |
| Blends | 5 g + extras | Synergy | No proven synergy; costly | $1.33–$1.67 |

### 3.2 Creatine HCl

Creatine hydrochloride is a simple salt with higher aqueous solubility than
monohydrate. The marketing inference — better solubility, better absorption, so a
lower dose works — is not supported.

A critical review of alternative forms concluded that there is no evidence HCl is
absorbed more effectively, promotes greater muscle creatine retention at equivalent
doses, or is as effective at lower doses [R03]. Two recent trials agree:

- In elite handball and softball players, CrM and Cr-HCl produced similar
  neuromuscular and body-composition outcomes at low doses; only CrM improved the
  fat-free mass index [R07].
- A 2024 trial comparing Cr-HCl with two monohydrate strategies found no advantage
  for HCl on strength, hypertrophy, or hormones [R08].

The dose arithmetic is also unfavorable. A 750 mg HCl capsule supplies about 587 mg
creatine (78.2% of the salt mass), so a 5 g monohydrate dose requires about 7.5
capsules (8.5 for 5 g of pure creatine). At $29.99 for 72 capsules, that is about
**$3.12 per 5 g** — roughly 21 times the cheapest
monohydrate.

**Verdict:** not superior, much more expensive, and harder to dose. There is no
reason to choose it.

### 3.3 Buffered creatine (Kre-Alkalyn)

Buffered creatine is monohydrate adjusted to a high pH (marketed as pH 12) to
increase stability and reduce "bloat". The direct trial compared low-dose
Kre-Alkalyn (1.5 g/day), high-dose Kre-Alkalyn (20 g/day for 7 days, then 5 g/day),
and monohydrate (same loading protocol).

Muscle creatine increased in all groups over time, but the low-dose buffered group
gained least, and the pairwise comparison favoured monohydrate (p = 0.053). There
were no advantages in strength, body composition, anaerobic capacity, or side
effects. The authors concluded that buffered creatine is not more efficacious or
safer [R04].

**Verdict:** the pH story does not translate into a benefit. It is an expensive way
to buy monohydrate.

### 3.4 Creatine ethyl ester

CEE was designed to improve absorption by esterifying creatine. In the gut it is
unstable and converts to creatinine. In a direct trial, CEE produced **lower** serum
creatine than monohydrate despite being given at twice the recommended dose, raised
serum creatinine more, and did not improve body composition or performance beyond
training [R05; R06].

**Verdict:** avoid.

### 3.5 Creatine nitrate

Creatine nitrate combines creatine with nitrate. Dietary nitrate has independent
evidence for endurance and blood-flow effects, but the combination product often
contains relatively little creatine, and there is no adequate evidence that it beats
monohydrate for creatine-specific outcomes [R03].

**Verdict:** not recommended. If nitrate is the goal, beetroot juice is better
studied.

### 3.6 Citrate, malate, and magnesium chelate

These forms are more soluble or claim better absorption. There is no body of
comparative trials showing superiority, and the salt fraction is lower, so more
grams are needed for the same creatine dose.

**Verdict:** insufficient evidence; not worth a premium.

### 3.7 Gummies and liquids

Gummies are the fastest-growing format and the weakest on quality control. In a 2025
independent testing programme, **4 of 6 popular gummies contained little or no
creatine**; all tested powders passed [R46]. Gummies that did contain creatine had
elevated creatinine, indicating degradation during manufacturing or storage. Cost
per 5 g is $1.33–$2.22, and a serving can carry 5 g of sugar.

Liquid creatine has a stability problem: creatine degrades to creatinine in
solution, especially with heat and acidity [R02; R53]. A liquid product with a long
shelf life is a chemistry contradiction.

**Verdict:** a convenience trade-off only if you will not otherwise take creatine.
Choose a certified product and accept the cost.

### 3.8 Blends

Multi-ingredient products such as creatine + HMB, creatine + tart cherry, or
creatine + vitamin D are marketed as synergistic. The additional ingredients may
have their own evidence, but there is no consistent evidence that they enhance
creatine's effect, and the blend makes dose verification harder.

In the product database, Transparent Labs Creatine HMB costs $1.67 per 5 g of
creatine and Legion Recharge costs $1.33 — 3–11× a plain monohydrate powder. If you
want HMB or tart cherry, buy them separately so each can be evaluated on its own
evidence and dose.

**Verdict:** unnecessary for most people; buy monohydrate.

### 3.9 Micronised creatine and Creapure

**Micronisation** reduces particle size, improving mixability and mouthfeel. It
does not meaningfully improve solubility in a way that increases tissue uptake, and
it does not increase muscle creatine retention [R02; R03]. It is a convenience
feature, often free at the budget end, and not worth a premium.

**Creapure** is a branded German monohydrate specified at very high purity and used
in much of the foundational research [R02]. It is a positive ingredient-level signal,
not a finished-product certification: a product can use Creapure and still be
manufactured or stored poorly. Other suppliers can also meet compendial purity
standards.

## 4. Exercise performance: strength, power, sprint, endurance

### 4.1 Strength

The evidence is consistent in direction and modest in magnitude.

| Synthesis | Population | Outcome | Pooled effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 23 RCTs, 2024 [R09] | Adults under 50 | Upper-body 1RM | **+4.43 kg** (95% CI 3.12–5.75) |
| 23 RCTs, 2024 [R09] | Adults under 50 | Lower-body 1RM | **+11.35 kg** |
| 69 studies, 2025 [R10] | Mixed adults | Bench press | **+1.43 kg** |
| 69 studies, 2025 [R10] | Mixed adults | Squat | **+5.64 kg** |

The difference between the two syntheses is instructive. The 2024 review restricted
inclusion to adults under 50 and to controlled resistance-training protocols; the
2025 review included 69 studies with broader populations and outcomes. Broader
inclusion usually shrinks pooled effects because it adds noise and heterogeneous
protocols. Both agree that creatine adds a small but real strength advantage.

For an individual, a 4 kg bench press gain after months of training is meaningful
but not transformative. It is the kind of difference that can separate a stalled
programme from progress, not the kind that replaces years of work.

### 4.2 Power and sprint

| Outcome | Pooled effect | Certainty | Source |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Wingate peak power | **+47.81 W** (95% CI 15.55–80.06) | Moderate | [R10] |
| Vertical jump | **+1.48 cm** | Moderate | [R10] |
| Repeated-sprint mean power | SMD **0.61** | Low | [R34] |
| Repeated-sprint peak power | SMD 0.41 (not significant) | Low | [R34] |

The pattern fits the mechanism: creatine helps repeated high-intensity efforts more
than a single maximal effort. In repeated-sprint tests, the benefit tends to appear
in later sprints, when phosphocreatine depletion would otherwise degrade performance.
Peak power in a single sprint is less consistently improved.

Older narrative syntheses describe typical acute improvements of 5–15% for maximal
power and strength, 1–5% for single-effort sprint performance, and 5–15% for work
during repeated sprints [R60]. These ranges are useful for expectations, not
guarantees.

### 4.3 Endurance

Creatine is not an endurance ergogenic in the usual sense. It does not raise VO2max
or improve steady-state aerobic performance in most trials [R33; R16]. It may help
with surges, attacks, finishing sprints, and repeated high-intensity efforts within
an endurance event — the "surges and sprints" use case [R33].

For a road cyclist or marathon runner, the realistic benefit is not a faster average
pace; it is a better ability to respond to a move or finish a race. For a team-sport
athlete, the repeated-sprint benefit is more relevant.

### 4.4 Recovery

Creatine may reduce markers of muscle damage and improve recovery between bouts,
but the evidence is less consistent than for strength [R16; R02]. It has been studied
for reducing muscle loss during immobilisation and supporting rehabilitation, with
promising but limited data [R01].

**Do not expect creatine to replace sleep, protein, or sensible programming.** It
is not an anti-inflammatory drug and it does not reliably reduce soreness in
everyone.

### 4.5 Who responds best in performance terms

- People performing short, repeated, high-intensity efforts.
- People with lower baseline muscle creatine (vegetarians, some women, some older
  adults).
- People who are training consistently and eating enough protein.
- People who take an adequate dose for long enough to saturate stores.

It is less useful for pure steady-state endurance, for people already highly
saturated, and for anyone expecting an acute stimulant-like effect.

## 5. Muscle growth and body composition

### 5.1 Lean mass

The key finding is that creatine amplifies the response to resistance training; it
does not build muscle by itself.

| Comparison | Effect on lean mass | Source |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Creatine + resistance training vs training + placebo | **+1.14 kg** (95% CI 0.69–1.59) | [R12] |
| Creatine + resistance training, across ages | **+1.10 kg** (95% CI 0.56–1.65) | [R11] |
| Creatine without exercise | **+0.03 kg** (95% CI -0.65 to 0.70, not significant) | [R11] |
| Older adults + resistance training | **+1.18 kg** (95% CI 0.70–1.67) | [R14] |
| Postmenopausal women + resistance training | **+0.37 kg** (95% CI 0.05–0.69) | [R17] |

A gain of about 1 kg of lean mass over a training block is modest but real. It is
roughly the difference between a good and a very good response to a single mesocycle,
not a transformation.

### 5.2 Fat mass

Creatine is not a fat burner, but the pooled data show a small favorable shift:

- fat mass **-0.73 kg** (95% CI -1.34 to -0.11);
- body fat percentage **-0.88 points** (95% CI -1.66 to -0.11) [R12].

These effects are small and probably mediated by increased training volume and lean
mass rather than by a direct lipolytic mechanism.

### 5.3 Water and scale weight

Loading adds about **0.8–1.5 kg** within a week, mostly intracellular water
[R34; R40]. Long-term users may be 1–2 kg heavier than they would otherwise be,
partly water and partly lean mass. Fat mass does not increase.

This is important for weight-class athletes and for anyone who panics at a
two-kilogram scale jump in the first week. It is not "bloat" in the subcutaneous
sense, and it usually settles.

### 5.4 Dose and training interaction

A dose-response analysis suggested that about **7 g/day or 0.3 g/kg/day** may
produce about 1 kg more lean mass than training alone, and about 0.7 kg more fat
loss [R12]. Dose-response analyses across trials are observational and should not be
over-interpreted, but they support the idea that 3–5 g is a floor, not a ceiling,
for larger or more advanced trainees.

The non-negotiable variable is training. Creatine without resistance training does
not meaningfully increase lean mass [R11].

## 6. Cognition, brain health, and neurological conditions

### 6.1 Why the brain is different

Brain creatine is harder to raise than muscle creatine. The blood–brain barrier
expresses the creatine transporter, and brain creatine uptake is slower and less
responsive to standard doses. Higher doses or longer durations may be needed for
cognitive effects, and the optimal protocol is not established [R30; R23].

This is the central caveat for the "cognitive creatine" market: a 5 g dose that
saturates muscle may not meaningfully change brain creatine in a young healthy
person.

### 6.2 Healthy adults and memory

A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found a small overall memory benefit:

- overall **SMD 0.29** (95% CI 0.04–0.53), with substantial heterogeneity;
- older adults aged 66–76: **SMD 0.88** (95% CI 0.22–1.55);
- younger adults aged 11–31: **SMD 0.03** (95% CI -0.14 to 0.20, not significant)
  [R23].

The largest single trial to date (n=123, crossover, 5 g/day for 6 weeks) found a
small Bayesian signal favouring creatine, but the primary tasks were not
statistically significant (backward digit span p=0.064; reasoning p=0.327), and
side effects were reported more often with creatine [R25].

A 2025 systematic review of cognition in ageing found that 5 of 6 studies reported a
positive association, mostly in memory and attention, but the studies were
heterogeneous and mostly low quality [R24].

**Verdict:** a small, plausible memory benefit in older adults; not a general
cognitive enhancer.

### 6.3 Sleep deprivation and metabolic stress

Creatine is biologically most useful when energy metabolism is stressed. Small
trials suggest it may attenuate some cognitive decrements during sleep deprivation,
particularly on executive and memory tasks, but results are inconsistent and doses
vary [R30]. A 2026 pilot found no effect of creatine on cognitive or physiological
variables after acute sleep restriction [R64].

**Verdict:** interesting, not a substitute for sleep, and not a reliable
"cognitive insurance" product.

### 6.4 Depression

A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 trials (n=1,093) found a small effect:

- **SMD -0.34** (95% CI -0.68 to 0.00), equivalent to about 2.2 points on the
  17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale;
- below the minimal important difference of 3.0 points;
- very low certainty, substantial heterogeneity, and evidence of bias favouring
  creatine [R26].

Some individual trials, particularly adjunctive use with SSRIs or CBT, show larger
effects. But the average effect is not clinically important, and creatine is not a
treatment for depression.

**Verdict:** emerging adjunctive research; not a substitute for evidence-based
mental health care.

### 6.5 Traumatic brain injury and concussion

Small trials suggest that creatine may improve or accelerate outcomes after mild
TBI, and a U.S. Department of Defense review calls the early evidence "compelling"
but notes that **no large prospective placebo-controlled trial has established
efficacy** and there are no clinical guidelines for creatine in TBI [R27].

A 2026 pilot in adults with persistent post-concussion symptoms found no significant
between-group changes after 8 weeks, though effect sizes were moderate and the study
was underpowered [R59].

**Verdict:** promising but unproven; specialist care is required. Do not self-treat
a concussion with creatine.

### 6.6 Parkinson's disease

A meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (n=1,339) found no significant benefit on total, mental,
ADL, or motor UPDRS scores [R28]. A large trial was stopped for futility. Creatine
is not a Parkinson's treatment.

One secondary observation deserves mention: in a large Parkinson's trial, participants
who consumed more than 300 mg/day of caffeine had faster disease progression. This
is observational and hypothesis-generating, not a reason to avoid coffee or
creatine [R28].

### 6.7 Other neurological and psychiatric conditions

Small studies have examined creatine in anxiety, PTSD, bipolar depression, and
neurodegenerative disease. The evidence is too limited to recommend it for any of
these outside research or specialist care [R29; R30]. In bipolar disorder, there is
a theoretical concern about mood destabilisation, and clinician involvement is
prudent.

## 7. Other health outcomes

### 7.1 Bone

Creatine does not increase bone mineral density in postmenopausal women or older
adults. A 2-year RCT in 237 postmenopausal women found no effect on femoral neck,
total hip, or lumbar spine BMD [R18]. A meta-analysis in older adults likewise found
no BMD effect [R14].

There were secondary signals in the 2-year trial: creatine maintained femoral neck
section modulus and buckling ratio, geometric measures related to bone strength, and
improved 80 m walking time [R18]. These are hypothesis-generating, not a reason to
use creatine as an osteoporosis intervention.

### 7.2 Sarcopenia, frailty, and physical function

In older adults, creatine plus resistance training increases lean tissue mass by
about **1.18 kg** and upper-body strength by a small amount (SMD 0.24) [R14]. A 2026
three-level meta-analysis found a small strength benefit (g=0.31) but did not find
significant effects on muscle mass or physical function, with low-to-very-low
certainty [R15].

The honest summary: creatine is a reasonable adjunct to resistance training in
healthy older adults, with a modest strength benefit. It is not a standalone
treatment for sarcopenia or frailty, and frail clinical populations need supervised
programmes.

### 7.3 Glucose and diabetes

Creatine has been studied for glucose disposal and glycaemic control, with mixed and
limited results [R16]. There is no basis for using it as a diabetes treatment. People
with diabetes should monitor glucose and renal function with a clinician, especially
because creatinine-based eGFR is confounded by creatine.

### 7.4 Injury, rehabilitation, and immobilisation

Creatine may reduce muscle loss during immobilisation and support rehabilitation,
but the trials are small [R01]. It is not a substitute for physiotherapy or a
reason to rush return to sport.

### 7.5 Pregnancy

Creatine is being studied as a potential intervention in pregnancy, but human safety
and efficacy data are insufficient for routine use [R21]. Do not self-supplement
during pregnancy or breastfeeding without clinician guidance.

### 7.6 Summary of health outcomes

| Outcome | Evidence | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength and power | Multiple meta-analyses | Established, modest |
| Lean mass with training | Multiple meta-analyses | Established, about 1 kg |
| Fat mass | Small pooled effect | Emerging, minor |
| Memory in older adults | Meta-analysis, low certainty | Emerging, small |
| Memory in young adults | Largest trial null | Unsupported for general use |
| Depression | Very low certainty, below MID | Emerging adjunct only |
| TBI | Small trials, no large RCT | Emerging, not a treatment |
| Parkinson's | No benefit | Unsupported |
| Bone density | No effect | Unsupported |
| Sarcopenia function | Uncertain | Emerging adjunct |
| Diabetes | Insufficient | Not recommended as treatment |
| Pregnancy | Insufficient | Avoid without clinician |

## 8. Dosage, loading, timing, and consistency

### 8.1 The core protocol

| Protocol | Dose | Time to saturation | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maintenance | **3–5 g/day** (0.03 g/kg) | About 28 days | Default for most people |
| Loading + maintenance | **20–25 g/day** (0.3 g/kg) in 4–5 doses for 5–7 days, then 3–5 g/day | About 5–7 days | Competition or testing within a week |
| Low-dose | 3 g/day | About 28 days | Smaller adults, sensitive stomachs |

A body-weight table and practical mixing guidance are in the
[dosage guide](../docs/dosage-guide.md).

### 8.2 Loading is optional

Loading does not produce a larger long-term effect; it compresses the timeline. The
cost of a 7-day load at 20 g/day is only about $4–$6 with a budget powder. The real
cost is GI discomfort and the hassle of four doses a day.

**Recommendation:** load only if you need saturation this week. Otherwise start at
5 g/day and be patient for four weeks.

### 8.3 Timing

Pre-workout, post-workout, with breakfast, or before bed all produce similar
adaptations in controlled comparisons [R55; R56; R57]. Post-exercise dosing has a
modest mechanistic rationale but no reliable advantage in trials.

**Recommendation:** attach creatine to a daily habit and stop optimising the clock.
Consistency is the active ingredient.

### 8.4 Consistency and missed doses

Muscle creatine declines slowly after stopping; full depletion takes weeks. A missed
day is not a crisis. Take the missed dose when you remember, do not double up, and
resume the habit.

### 8.5 Cycling

There is no pharmacological rationale for cycling creatine on and off. Continuous
use at recommended doses has been studied for years without evidence of harm
[R01; R02]. Cycling is a solution to a problem that does not exist.

### 8.6 Mixing and storage

- Creatine solubility is about 14 g/L at 20 °C; warm water dissolves more.
- Dry powder is stable for years.
- In solution, creatine converts to creatinine faster with heat and acidity: about
  4% at pH 5.5, 12% at pH 4.5, and 21% at pH 3.5 after 3 days at 25 °C [R02; R53].
- Mix and drink within the session; do not prepare a week of drinks.
- Acidic juice is fine if consumed promptly; a 2025 study confirmed temperature is
  the dominant stability factor [R54].

### 8.7 Dose in special groups

| Group | Dose | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Older adults | 5 g/day | Combine with resistance training |
| Women | 3–5 g/day | Same biology; often 3 g is enough at lower body mass |
| Postmenopausal women | 5 g/day | Small lean-mass and strength benefit with training |
| Vegetarians/vegans | 3–5 g/day | Lower baseline; likely greater response |
| Large athletes | 5–10 g/day | Maintenance scales with muscle mass |
| Adolescents | Clinician decision | Short-term data only |

## 9. Safety

### 9.1 Overall adverse events

The largest safety synthesis pooled 685 creatine studies and 652 placebo studies:
12,839 creatine users and 13,452 placebo users, average dose 0.166 g/kg/day (about
12.5 g/day), mean duration 65 days, up to 14 years [R35].

- Any side effect: **13.7% vs 13.2%** of studies, not significant.
- GI issues: **5.51% vs 4.05%** of participants, not significant.
- Cramping or muscle pain: **0.52% vs 0.07%**, not significant.

The direction of the point estimates is worth acknowledging: creatine may cause a
small increase in GI symptoms and cramps. The effect is mild and manageable, not a
reason to avoid the supplement.

### 9.2 Kidney function

Creatine is converted to creatinine at about 1–2% of the body pool per day [R53].
Supplementing creatine raises serum creatinine even when kidney function is normal.

Two 2026 meta-analyses:

| Marker | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Serum creatinine | **+0.14 mg/dL** (95% CI 0.05–0.22) [R38] |
| eGFR from creatinine | **-10.75 mL/min/1.73 m²** (95% CI -17.48 to -4.02) [R38] |
| eGFR from chromium-51 EDTA | **+5.89 mL/min/1.73 m²** (95% CI -0.30 to 12.08, not significant) [R38] |
| Urea, albuminuria, proteinuria | No significant change [R37; R38] |
| eGFR from creatinine, separate meta-analysis | -5.20 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI -15.00 to 4.60, not significant) [R37] |

The tracer result is decisive: when GFR is measured directly rather than estimated
from creatinine, creatine does not impair it. A 12-week trial in resistance-trained
men on a high-protein diet found no change in chromium-51 EDTA clearance, creatinine
clearance, urea, electrolytes, proteinuria, or albuminuria [R39].

**The clinical trap:** a creatine user can be misclassified as having chronic kidney
disease because automated eGFR assumes creatinine comes only from muscle. This is a
diagnostic artefact. If kidney function matters, tell the clinician and lab, and ask
for cystatin C or a direct tracer measurement.

**Pre-existing kidney disease:** trials largely excluded these patients. Case reports
exist but are confounded. Avoid unsupervised use; nephrology involvement is required
[R36; R40].

### 9.3 Liver

No controlled trial has shown a clinically meaningful adverse effect on liver
enzymes. A large US population analysis found no association between dietary
creatine and liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, or steatosis [R63]. Safety reviews agree
[R40]. People with liver disease were largely excluded from trials, so the evidence
applies to healthy livers.

### 9.4 Hydration and thermoregulation

Creatine increases intracellular water, not extracellular fluid. Total body water may
rise by 0.8–1.5 L during loading, but plasma volume, sweat rate, electrolytes, and
core temperature do not worsen [R40; R41]. A meta-analysis of 10 studies found no
evidence that creatine impairs heat dissipation or fluid balance [R41].

Some data suggest creatine may help maintain hydration and reduce cramping, though
this is less certain [R40]. The "creatine causes dehydration" claim is not supported.

### 9.5 Body weight

Loading adds about 0.8–1.5 kg in a week; long-term use is associated with a 1–2 kg
increase [R34; R40]. This is water and lean mass, not fat.

### 9.6 Gastrointestinal effects

GI symptoms are dose-dependent and more common during loading. Split the dose, take
it with food, use cold water, and reduce to 3–5 g/day if needed. There is no
consistent evidence that HCl or buffered creatine causes less GI upset; the buffered
trial found no reduction in side effects [R04].

### 9.7 Hair loss and DHT

The claim originates from a 2009 crossover trial in 20 rugby players: DHT rose 56%
after 7 days of loading and remained 40% above baseline after 21 days, with a 36%
rise in the DHT:testosterone ratio [R44]. The study did not measure hair.

A 2025 RCT gave 38 men 5 g/day for 12 weeks and measured DHT, testosterone, the
DHT:T ratio, hair density, follicular unit count, and cumulative hair thickness.
There were no significant differences between creatine and placebo for any hormone
or hair outcome [R43].

**Verdict:** unsupported. A transient DHT change at loading doses is possible, but
it has not been shown to affect hair, and the only trial designed to measure hair
found no effect.

### 9.8 Cancer

No human evidence shows that creatine increases cancer risk [R40]. Animal and in
vitro signals are mixed and do not establish human risk.

### 9.9 Pregnancy, breastfeeding, and minors

Human safety data in pregnancy and breastfeeding are insufficient; do not
self-supplement [R21; R40]. Short-term use in supervised adolescent athletes has not
raised safety signals, but long-term data are lacking [R01; R02]. Minors should not
use creatine without guardian and clinician involvement.

### 9.10 Bipolar disorder

Case reports and clinical guidance suggest caution because creatine's effects on
brain energy metabolism could destabilise mood [R40]. The evidence is case-level,
but clinician involvement is prudent.

### 9.11 Medications

A 2026 evidence-mapping review found **no controlled human studies** of
creatine–drug interactions and no established moderate- or high-severity
interaction. Signals are minor and theoretical, mostly renal [R42].

| Drug class | Concern | Advice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NSAIDs | Additive kidney stress, especially with dehydration | Occasional use fine; chronic use needs review |
| Diuretics | Fluid/electrolyte shifts, kidney load | Discuss with prescriber |
| ACE inhibitors / ARBs | Renal haemodynamics | Generally compatible; monitor if kidney disease |
| Nephrotoxic drugs (cyclosporine, lithium, aminoglycosides) | Theoretical additive risk | Avoid unsupervised |
| Caffeine/stimulants | Possible attenuation at ≥5 mg/kg; no consistent harm | Normal coffee is fine |
| Parkinson's medication | Secondary caffeine signal | Do not change treatment; discuss with neurologist |

### 9.12 Contamination

The largest safety risk is a poorly made product, not the molecule. Heavy metals,
underdosing, and degradation to creatinine have been documented, especially in
gummies. See [product quality](#11-product-quality-contamination-testing-and-certification).

### 9.13 Risk summary

| Concern | Evidence | Bottom line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GI upset | Consistent, mild | Manageable |
| Weight gain | Consistent, 0.8–2 kg | Water and lean mass |
| Serum creatinine rise | Consistent | Expected, not injury |
| Measured GFR decline | Absent with tracer | Lab artefact |
| Kidney damage in healthy people | Not supported | Safe at recommended doses |
| Kidney disease progression | Insufficient | Avoid unless supervised |
| Liver harm | Not supported | Safe in healthy livers |
| Dehydration/cramps | Not supported | No extra fluid needed |
| Hair loss | Not supported | Myth |
| Cancer | Not supported | No human signal |
| Pregnancy | Insufficient | Avoid without clinician |
| Drug interactions | No controlled studies | Caution with kidney-stressing drugs |

## 10. Sex, age, and diet differences

### 10.1 Women

Women have lower baseline muscle creatine than men, partly because of lower body
mass and dietary intake. They respond to supplementation, but the pooled lean-mass
estimate is smaller and less precise: **+1.46 kg in men vs +0.29 kg in women (not
significant)** in one meta-analysis [R11]. This is likely a power problem — women
were under-represented — rather than evidence of no benefit.

A 2026 multilevel meta-analysis in exercising women found a small positive average
effect across outcomes, with performance and body composition positive and
physiological outcomes uncertain; certainty was low to very low [R22].

Safety data are reassuring: a systematic review and meta-analysis found no increase
in total adverse events, weight gain, renal or hepatic complications in women
[R19]. Creatine does not appear to worsen menstrual symptoms; a small RCT found
increased total body water in the luteal phase without a body-mass difference [R20].

**Postmenopausal women:** about **+0.37 kg lean mass** and **+7.5 kg leg press**
with resistance training, with no bone-density effect [R17; R18].

**Pregnancy:** insufficient evidence; avoid without clinician guidance [R21].

### 10.2 Men

Men show larger absolute lean-mass gains in pooled analyses, but this is confounded
by body size, baseline creatine, and training. The mechanism is the same; the dose
may need to be higher for larger men (5–10 g/day) [R01].

### 10.3 Older adults

Older adults benefit from creatine plus resistance training:

- lean tissue mass **+1.18 kg** (95% CI 0.70–1.67);
- upper-body strength SMD **0.24** (95% CI 0.05–0.43);
- lower-body strength and physical function: not consistently significant
  [R14; R15].

Bone mineral density does not improve, though bone geometry may [R18].

Creatine is a reasonable adjunct in healthy older adults who resistance train. It is
not a standalone treatment for sarcopenia, frailty, or osteoporosis.

### 10.4 Adolescents

Short-term use in supervised adolescent athletes appears safe, but long-term data
are lacking and the ethical bar is higher [R02]. Food, training, and sleep come
first. Creatine is not a default supplement for teenagers.

### 10.5 Vegetarians and vegans

Vegetarians have lower muscle creatine (about 10–15% lower total creatine, with
larger plasma differences) and respond to supplementation with greater increases,
sometimes exceeding omnivore levels [R31]. Performance evidence is mixed, but the
storage rationale is strong. This group is among the most likely to benefit.

### 10.6 Non-responders

Response varies with baseline creatine, fibre type, and muscle mass [R32]. The
"30% non-responder" figure is not a population estimate. If you have taken 5 g/day
for a month and see no change in training performance, the likely explanations are:
you were already near saturation, your training did not test the relevant energy
system, or your dose was too low for your body mass.

## 11. Product quality: contamination, testing, and certification

### 11.1 What can go wrong

| Problem | What it means | Documented? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Creatinine | Degradation product; indicates poor manufacturing or storage | Yes, 44% of samples >100 mg/kg in a 2011 survey [R45] |
| Dicyandiamide / DHO | Manufacturing by-products | Yes, above regulatory limits in some samples [R45] |
| Heavy metals | Lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury | Mostly low; occasional lead exceedance in gummies [R45; R46] |
| Underdosing | Less creatine than the label claims | Yes, especially gummies [R46; R47] |
| Microbial contamination | Poor hygiene | Possible in unsealed or poor-quality products |
| Adulteration | Unlisted stimulants or other ingredients | Rare in single-ingredient creatine; a risk in blends |

### 11.2 Independent testing results

- **2011 Food Chemistry survey (33 products):** creatinine was the major organic
  contaminant, exceeding 100 mg/kg in 44% of samples; about 15% had DHO above the
  detection limit; some had dicyandiamide >50 mg/kg; only mercury was detectable
  among heavy metals, below 1 mg/kg [R45].
- **SuppCo 2025:** all tested powders passed identity and potency; 4 of 6 gummies
  contained little or no creatine; one gummy exceeded California's Prop 65 lead
  limit at 0.99 µg/serving [R46].
- **ConsumerLab 2026:** one product exceeded the creatinine limit; cost per 5 g
  ranged from $0.13 to $1.85, and higher cost did not predict higher quality [R48].

### 11.3 Certifications

| Programme | What it verifies | Frequency | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | ~290 banned substances, label content, formulation review, facility inspection, annual audits | Ongoing | Drug-tested athletes |
| **Informed Sport** | 285+ banned substances by ISO 17025 methods, every batch before release | Every batch | Drug-tested athletes |
| **Informed Choice** | Retail monitoring and periodic banned-substance testing | Periodic retail samples | General quality signal |
| **NSF Contents Certified** | Label content and contaminants, not sport-banned substances | Periodic | Purity assurance |
| **USP Verified** | Label content, contaminants, manufacturing | Periodic | General quality signal |

Sources: [R49; R51; R52].

Certification is a **risk-reduction** tool, not an efficacy claim. It does not make
the creatine work better, and its absence does not prove contamination.

### 11.4 Micronisation and Creapure

Micronisation improves mixability, not absorption [R02; R03]. Creapure is a
high-purity branded ingredient and a positive signal, but it is not a finished-product
certification [R02].

### 11.5 Blends and proprietary formulas

Blends make it harder to verify the creatine dose and add ingredients with separate,
often weaker evidence. They are usually more expensive per gram of creatine. Buy
single-ingredient monohydrate unless a specific co-ingredient is independently
justified.

### 11.6 Storage

Dry powder is stable for years. In solution, creatine degrades to creatinine faster
with heat and acidity [R02; R53; R54]. Keep the tub dry and sealed; mix drinks fresh.

## 14. Who benefits most, and who should be cautious

### 14.1 Benefits most

1. **Strength and power athletes** — weightlifters, sprinters, team-sport athletes,
   throwers, combat athletes.
2. **People doing resistance training for muscle** — the lean-mass effect requires
   training; creatine amplifies it.
3. **Older adults who resistance train** — modest strength and lean-mass benefit,
   with a good safety profile.
4. **Vegetarians and vegans** — lower baseline stores, larger response.
5. **Postmenopausal women** — small lean-mass and leg-press benefit with training.
6. **People with low baseline creatine or high type II fibre proportion** — more
   room to respond.
7. **Possibly older adults seeking cognitive support** — small memory effect, low
   certainty; not a guaranteed benefit.

### 14.2 May benefit modestly

- Endurance athletes who need surges or finishing sprints.
- People recovering from immobilisation or injury (adjunct, not treatment).
- People with sleep deprivation or metabolic stress (small, inconsistent cognitive
  effects).

### 14.3 Should exercise caution

- **Chronic kidney disease, dialysis, transplant, single kidney** — avoid unless a
  nephrologist agrees.
- **People taking nephrotoxic drugs, lithium, cyclosporine, or regular high-dose
  NSAIDs** — clinician review.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** — insufficient evidence; avoid without guidance.
- **Bipolar disorder** — case-level concern about mood destabilisation.
- **Under 18** — guardian and clinician involvement; not a default.
- **Liver disease** — trials excluded; clinician guidance.
- **Diabetes or hypertension with kidney involvement** — monitor renal function;
  interpret creatinine cautiously.
- **Anyone with unexplained creatinine or liver enzyme elevation** — investigate
  before supplementing.
- **Weight-class athletes** — account for 1–2 kg of water weight.

### 14.4 Does not need it

- People who do not resistance train and are not athletically active.
- People who already eat a creatine-rich diet and are satisfied with performance.
- Anyone expecting weight loss, a stimulant effect, or a cure for a disease.

## 15. Is creatine genuinely beneficial? The verdict

**Yes, for specific outcomes, at a low cost, with a good safety profile — and with
much weaker evidence than the marketing implies for everything else.**

The strongest claims are:

1. **Strength and power:** consistent, moderate-size effects across meta-analyses.
2. **Lean mass with resistance training:** about 1 kg additional lean mass and a
   small fat-mass reduction.
3. **Safety in healthy adults:** adverse events similar to placebo, including
   long-duration studies.

The weakest claims are:

1. **Cognitive enhancement in healthy young people:** small, inconsistent, and not
   statistically robust in the largest trial.
2. **Fat loss:** minor and secondary to training.
3. **Bone density, depression, TBI, Parkinson's:** unproven or negative.
4. **Superiority of alternative forms:** not supported.

The practical conclusion is unglamorous: **buy plain creatine monohydrate, take
3–5 g every day, train hard, and do not pay for HCl, buffered creatine, blends, or
gummies unless convenience is worth 6–21× the price.**

## 16. Limitations of this review

1. **Single reviewer, AI-assisted.** No duplicate screening or extraction.
2. **Paywalled testing detail.** ConsumerLab's full product-level results are
   member-only; only public summaries are cited.
3. **Publication and funding bias.** Many trials are small and some industry-funded.
4. **Heterogeneity.** Populations, doses, and outcomes differ; pooled estimates are
   averages.
5. **Price volatility.** Prices and certifications change; the dataset is a dated
   snapshot.
6. **Purposive product sample.** Twenty products illustrate the market; they are not
   a census.
7. **No new laboratory assays.** Contamination conclusions rely on published tests.
8. **Generalisation.** Frail, pregnant, paediatric, and clinical populations are
   under-represented.

Full methods, including the search strategy and grading scheme, are in
[`docs/methodology.md`](../docs/methodology.md).

## 17. Conclusion

Creatine monohydrate is one of the few supplements that survives contact with the
evidence. It does so because the effect is real, the mechanism is understood, the
dose is simple, the cost is low, and the safety data are unusually deep. But the
effect is modest and conditional: it amplifies training, it does not replace it, and
it is not a cognitive or longevity panacea.

The most important consumer insight is not about creatine at all. It is about the
market around it: the same molecule costs **$0.15 or $3.12 per 5 g**, and the
expensive versions are not better. Buy the powder, take it daily, and spend the
difference on food, coaching, or sleep.
